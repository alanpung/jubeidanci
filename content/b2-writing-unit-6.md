answer: "Examinations remain the most common method of assessment because they appear fair and efficient. || Every candidate faces the same questions under similar conditions, which reduces the influence of personal connections and subjective judgement. Moreover, exams provide clear evidence of academic achievement and help universities select students. However, this system has serious drawbacks, since teaching often narrows to exam techniques and memorisation rather than deep understanding. Yet they encourage teaching to the test, memorisation and neglect of creativity. Alternatives such as portfolios may be costly and subjective. Therefore a mixed system works best, combining exams for core knowledge with continuous assessment of analysis, collaboration and original work. Clear criteria keep such assessment credible. Clear marking criteria and transparency also help students trust the results."
acceptAlso: "Standardised tests create an impression of equality and are relatively simple to organise. || They also give students a clear goal and measurable feedback. Unfortunately, when results determine nearly everything, classrooms focus on predicting answers instead of asking questions. This harms curiosity and deep learning. Coursework offers a broader picture but can favour students with supportive families. Oral presentations test communication that written papers cannot capture. || Group projects reveal cooperation, although they must be marked individually to remain fair. No single format can measure intelligence completely, so variety is the safest principle. I suggest using several methods together, so no single score defines a person's ability. [writing]
text: "Write this passage in English"
sentence: "Write about studying for understanding in English. || Key vocabulary hints: understanding, memorisation, transfer, rehearsal, recall"
answer: "Learning for understanding is more effective than memorisation because connected knowledge is easier to recall and apply. || When students grasp why a formula or argument works, they can transfer it to unfamiliar problems. In contrast, memorised facts learned for an examination are often forgotten within weeks. Moreover, deep understanding encourages genuine interest, whereas repetition quickly becomes boring. For instance, a student who can explain why a historical event happened is more likely to remember its details than one who memorised a date list. Teachers can check understanding by asking pupils to teach a concept back to them. || However, some basic information does need to be remembered, such as vocabulary or key dates, so memorisation is not entirely useless. Consequently, the two approaches should work together rather than compete. On the other hand, teachers can promote understanding through explanation, examples and discussion. In my view, education should prioritise meaning, with rehearsal used only as a supporting tool. || When pupils explain their reasoning aloud, misconceptions become visible and can be corrected immediately."
core: "Understanding beats memorisation because connected knowledge is easier to remember and apply to new situations, while exam facts fade quickly and repetition kills interest. Still, vocabulary and dates require some recall, so both methods can coexist. Teachers encourage depth through examples and explanation, asking students to reformulate ideas. Meaning should lead, with rehearsal as a supporting tool. This balance makes learning both durable and efficient. Repeated practice in varied situations then strengthens genuine understanding and memory."
acceptAlso: "Students who merely repeat information may pass a test yet understand little. || True learning involves explaining relationships, questioning assumptions and using knowledge in different contexts. This takes more time and feels harder, but it lasts longer. Rote learning remains useful for automatic skills such as spelling or multiplication, where slow thinking would be exhausting. The mistake is to confuse fluency with understanding and stop asking why. || Varied practice forces students to transfer knowledge, which strengthens memory naturally. I believe the best method is to introduce ideas clearly, then revisit them through varied practice, ensuring that recall supports understanding instead of replacing it. Examinations should include unfamiliar problems so that pupils are rewarded for insight rather than rehearsed answers. || Good teaching therefore combines clear explanation with repeated opportunities to use an idea in unfamiliar contexts and in conversation with other learners."
srsWords: "understanding" "memorisation" "transfer" "rehearsal" "recall"
