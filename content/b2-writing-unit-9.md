core: "Saving regularly matters because it creates a safety net for unexpected costs and allows small amounts to grow steadily through compound interest over many years. It also trains people to choose long-term security over impulse spending, which reduces anxiety and builds confidence. Moreover, savings encourage independence by removing the need to borrow from relatives. Consequently, a person who saves consistently can face the future with greater freedom, resilience and respect for the value of their own daily effort."

However, advertising deliberately blurs this boundary by presenting luxury items as symbols of identity and success and by linking them to happiness. Consequently, consumers often buy products they neither need nor truly enjoy, and regret follows once the excitement fades. || Moreover, social media intensifies the problem because people constantly compare their possessions with those of others. On the other hand, a clear distinction between the two categories makes budgeting far easier and reduces impulsive decisions. Therefore, pausing to ask whether a purchase is necessary can save both money and emotional energy. || Ultimately, mindful consumption allows us to spend on what genuinely improves our lives rather than on temporary impressions."

core: "Needs such as food, shelter and transport support health and work, while wants are optional extras linked to comfort or status. Advertising blurs this line by presenting luxury goods as symbols of identity, while social media encourages constant comparison. As a result, people buy things they do not enjoy and later regret them. Distinguishing the two makes budgeting easier, reduces impulsive decisions and saves emotional energy. Mindful consumption directs money towards what genuinely improves life rather than chasing temporary impressions."

Sensible shoppers therefore set a budget, consult independent feedback, wait before confirming orders and check the seller's reputation. With alertness and discipline, the internet becomes a practical tool rather than a trap that drains our savings and creates lasting regret."

answer: "Price and value are related, but they are never the same thing. || Price is simply the amount of money a seller asks, whereas value is the usefulness and satisfaction a buyer gains over time. To begin with, a cheap jacket that tears after a month may cost far more than a durable one worn for years. Moreover, people often pay extra for a famous label, although the product itself may be identical to a cheaper alternative. However, this does not mean that expensive goods are always worthless; higher prices can reflect better materials, safer production and longer guarantees. || Consequently, sensible consumers look at the cost per use rather than the number on the shelf. On the other hand, a low price can signal poor quality or hidden costs such as repairs and wasted time. In addition, emotional value matters too, since a handmade gift may be priceless to its owner. Therefore, asking what we truly receive in exchange is more useful than hunting for the lowest figure. || Ultimately, real value lies in lasting benefit, not in temporary excitement."

core: "Price is what a seller charges, while value is the usefulness and satisfaction gained over time. A cheap item that breaks quickly may cost more than a durable one, and famous labels often add expense without improving quality. Lasting benefit matters more than a low number on a shelf."
